Introduction

  • An exchange economy corresponds to a situation in which several agents are initially endowed with consumption goods.

  • From a given basket of goods, an agent draws a certain level of “satisfaction” or utility which is measured by a utility function.

  • Agents are allowed to trade their initial endowments with each other, in a Walrasian market, to possibly increase their utility.

  • The efficiency of the market outcome can be assessed with the concept of Pareto optimality.

  • In this class we focus the simplest setup: 2 agents and 2 goods.

2x2 Economy

  • Two agents, A and B, and two goods, 1 and 2.

  • Initial endowments:

    • A endowed with quantities \(\omega_{1}^{A}\) of good 1, and \(\omega_{2}^{A}\) of good 2
    • B endowed with quantities \(\omega_{1}^{B}\) of good 1, and \(\omega_{2}^{B}\) of good 2

  • Aggregate resources of goods 1 and 2: \(\omega_{1} = \omega_{1}^{A} + \omega_{1}^{B}\), \(\omega_{2} = \omega_{2}^{A} + \omega_{2}^{B}\).

  • Feasible baskets for A: \((x_{1},x_{2})\) with \(x_{1} \ge 0\), \(x_{2} \ge 0\).

  • Feasible baskets for B: \((y_{1},y_{2})\) with \(y_{1} \ge 0\), \(y_{2} \ge 0\).

  • Feasible allocation: a pair of baskets \(\{(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\}\), such that \(x_{1} + y_{1} = \omega_{1}\), and \(x_{2} + y_{2} = \omega_{2}\)

  • The Edgeworth box represents feasible allocations.

Edgeworth Box

Walrasian Market

  • Good 1 can be traded (bought and sold) at price \(p_{1}\). Good 2 can be traded at price \(p_{2}\).

  • These prices are not parameters. Our objective is to determine the prices that clear the markets (s.t. demand = supply)

  • Given \(p_{1}\) and \(p_{2}\), A’s initial wealth is \(W_{A} = p_{1} \, \omega_{1}^{A} + p_{2} \, \omega_{2}^{A}\).

  • Feasible baskets under budget constraints for A: \((x_{1},x_{2})\) such that \(p_{1} x_{1} + p_{2} x_{2} \le W_{A}\)

    Optimally her budget is exhausted. She will pick an allocation on the budget line: \(p_{1} x_{1} + p_{2} x_{2} = W_{A}\). Idem for B.

  • Budget constraints partition the Edgeworth box.

Preferences

  • Individual preferences of A over baskets represented by the utility function, \(u^{A}\). A specific (feasible) basket \((x_{1},x_{2})\) yields a utility level \(u^{A}(x_{1},x_{2}) \in \mathbb{R}\). Idem for B.

  • The utility function should be strictly concave, and strictly increasing in each good, that is \(\frac{\partial u^{A}}{\partial x_{1}} > 0\), \(\frac{\partial u^{A}}{\partial x_{2}} > 0\), \(\frac{\partial^{2} u^{A}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}} < 0\), \(\frac{\partial^{2} u^{A}}{\partial x_{2}^{2}} < 0\).

  • Example: Cobb-Douglas utility function \[ u^{A}(x_{1},x_{2}) = x_{1}^{\alpha} x_{2}^{1-\alpha}, \quad u^{B}(y_{1},y_{2}) = y_{1}^{\beta} y_{2}^{1-\beta}, \] with \(\alpha \in [0,1]\), \(\beta \in [0,1]\).

  • One can represent iso-utility curves, that sets of baskets generating a given utility level, in the Edgeworth box

Pareto Optimality

  • Allocation \(\{(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\}\) dominates allocation \(\{(x_{1}',x_{2}'),(y_{1}',y_{2}')\}\) in the Pareto sense if \(u^{A}(x_{1},x_{2}) \ge u^{A}(x_{1}',x_{2}')\), and \(u^{B}(y_{1},y_{2}) \ge u^{B}(y_{1}',y_{2}')\), with at least one strict inequality, that is if both agents are better off with the first allocation.

  • A feasible allocation \(\{(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\}\) is Pareto optimal if it is not dominated by any feasible allocation in the Pareto sense.

    Put differently, any other feasible allocation would make (at least) one agent worse off.

  • Typically, there are multiple (a set of) Pareto optimal allocations.

Individual Optimization

  • Result: Under the strict concavity and increasing assumptions on \(u^{A}\), and for given prices \(p_{1}\) and \(p_{2}\), there exists a unique optimal basket \((x_{1},x_{2})\). It maximizes \(u^{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\) under the budget constraint.

  • Based on this existence and uniqueness result, one can define the demand function of A.

    The demand function of A maps each set of price \((p_{1},p_{2})\) to the optimal basket, \((x_{1}(p_{1},p_{2}),x_{2}(p_{1},p_{2}))\).

  • Idem for B.

Walrasian Equilibrium

Definition. A (Walrasian) equilibrium is \(\{(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*}),(y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*}),p_{1}^{*},p_{2}^{*}\}\) such that

  1. \((x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*})\) maximizes \(u^{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\) u.c \(p_{1}^{*} x_{1} + p_{2}^{*} x_{2} = p_{1}^{*} \omega_{1}^{A} + p_{2}^{*} \omega_{2}^{A}\)
  2. \((y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*})\) maximizes \(u^{B}(y_{1},y_{2})\) u.c \(p_{1}^{*} y_{1} + p_{2}^{*} y_{2} = p_{1}^{*} \omega_{1}^{B} + p_{2}^{*} \omega_{2}^{B}\)
  3. market clearing: \(x_{1}^{*} + y_{1}^{*} = \omega_{1}\) and \(x_{2}^{*} + y_{2}^{*} = \omega_{2}\)

Equilibrium prices are not uniquely determined - only up to a scalar one can choose a numeraire, for instance \(p_{2}^{*} = 1\).

First Welfare Theorem. If \(\{(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*}),(y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*}),p_{1}^{*},p_{2}^{*}\}\) is an equilibrium then \(\{(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*}),(y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*})\}\) is a Pareto optimal allocation.

Further (Important) Results

  • Second Welfare Theorem. If \(\{(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*}),(y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*})\}\) is a Pareto optimal allocation, then it is the allocation of an equilibrium with transfers.

  • Equilibrium + transfer = take 1000ac from A, give it to B, and let them trade out of their endowments + transfers.

    It means that taxes allow to reach the Pareto optimum we wish among the many.

  • All the results can be generalized to an economy with \(N \ge 2\) agents and \(L \ge 2\) goods.
